# User Interview Transcript 1

---

**Interviewer:** Thanks for making time. To start, can you tell me about the last group project you worked on?

**Student:** Yeah. It was for strategy. We had to analyze a company and make a recommendation. Pretty normal group project, honestly. We got assigned randomly, and I think there were four of us? Maybe five. I actually can't remember if one person kind of disappeared halfway through or if they were just quiet.

**Interviewer:** What happened once the group got assigned?

**Student:** We stayed after class for a bit and did the usual thing where everyone says, okay, I can do slides, I can do research, I can talk in the presentation. It always sounds organized in that first ten minutes. Then someone made a group chat, because that's just automatic now.

**Interviewer:** What platform was the chat on?

**Student:** iMessage for that one. Although sometimes it's GroupMe or WhatsApp if people have Androids. But the point is kind of the same.

**Interviewer:** Right, sorry, keep going.

**Student:** Yeah, so everything ends up in the chat. Not just planning, like literally everything. Task assignments, scheduling, random jokes, "I'm running late," screenshots from the professor, all mixed together. And at first that feels easy because nobody has to open another tool. But then after a couple days it's weirdly hard to tell what was actually decided.

**Interviewer:** Can you give me an example?

**Student:** Um, yeah. One time someone said they would do the company background section. Then later someone else started doing basically the same thing because they didn't see that message, or forgot it, or maybe thought it changed. I don't even know. We ended up with duplicate work, and then another part wasn't done because everyone assumed somebody else had it.

**Interviewer:** So was the main issue duplicate work?

**Student:** Kind of, but not only that. Duplicate work is more like a symptom. The bigger thing is you don't have a reliable picture of what's going on. You're always piecing it together from scattered messages.

**Interviewer:** What do you mean by "reliable picture"?

**Student:** Like if I asked, right now, who owns what, what's done, what's blocked, and what's still kind of fake-done... usually nobody can answer that cleanly unless it's the night before and everyone's panicking.

**Interviewer:** "Fake-done" is interesting. What does that mean?

**Student:** Oh, just when someone says "I got it" and what they mean is they opened the document once. Sorry, that sounds mean. But you know what I mean. It's not malicious. It's more like everyone is busy and wants to signal progress.

**Interviewer:** That's helpful. Walk me through the last time you realized things were off.

**Student:** I think it was maybe three or four days before the presentation. Someone asked in the chat if we had final numbers for the recommendation, and then it became obvious that we all had different assumptions about where the project was. One person thought the research was basically done, one person thought we were still dividing work, and I was like, wait, I thought the slide deck already existed.

**Interviewer:** And what happened next?

**Student:** Everyone started scrolling back. Which is kind of ridiculous when you think about it. That becomes the system: people searching old text messages like it's a project management database.

**Interviewer:** Did anyone suggest using a separate tool?

**Student:** Yeah, but usually that suggestion comes after things already feel messy. Someone says, maybe we should put this in Notion or Trello or something. Then people kind of agree in theory, but no one wants to be the person managing it.

**Interviewer:** Why not?

**Student:** Because it feels like extra work. Especially for a class project that's going to last, like, two weeks. People are willing to suffer through a messy chat longer than they're willing to set up a system.

**Interviewer:** That's interesting, because it sounds like the chat is still the center of gravity even when it isn't working.

**Student:** Yeah, exactly. Also, sorry, one thing I forgot. The scheduling part makes everything worse.

**Interviewer:** Tell me more about that.

**Student:** Because the same place where you're trying to assign work is also the place where six people are saying "I can do Tuesday after 4, not Wednesday, maybe Thursday, wait no I have practice." So the actual task decisions just get buried.

**Interviewer:** So scheduling is the real problem?

**Student:** I wouldn't say it's the only problem. It's more like it exposes the problem. Even if scheduling were easy, I still think we'd have the issue that decisions are living inside conversation instead of somewhere visible.

**Interviewer:** Got it. Did your group ever actually try Trello or Notion?

**Student:** We tried Trello once in a different class. It wasn't terrible. It just kind of died after the first week. People made cards, but then nobody updated them. And once it stopped reflecting reality, everyone went back to the chat and started asking each other directly.

**Interviewer:** Was that because Trello was too complicated?

**Student:** Not exactly. I think it was more that it asked for maintenance. Like, the information didn't update itself. If students have to remember one more thing, they just won't.

**Interviewer:** Makes sense. If something worked inside the chat, would that be better?

**Student:** Probably, yes. I mean, I don't know if "inside the chat" is the only answer, but it would remove one reason people ignore the tool.

**Interviewer:** When you say "inside the chat," do you mean like an AI assistant?

**Student:** Maybe? Although I don't think I would frame it that way first. I think I just want something that notices decisions and turns them into something clearer. Like, okay, Alex owns slides, Jamie owns research, deadline Thursday. Just make that visible without us having to copy it somewhere.

**Interviewer:** So almost like automatic summarization?

**Student:** Summarization, but more actionable than a summary. Because summaries are nice, but then you still have to do something with them.

**Interviewer:** What would make that feel trustworthy enough to use?

**Student:** If it was accurate. Which sounds obvious, but seriously, if it guessed wrong about who owns something, people would stop trusting it immediately. Also it can't be too annoying. If it's constantly popping into the chat, everyone would mute it.

**Interviewer:** Would you want it to assign tasks?

**Student:** No, I don't think so. That would feel weirdly aggressive. Maybe it can suggest what it thinks the decisions are, but I wouldn't want it acting like a manager.

**Interviewer:** Is there anything else that feels frustrating about group projects?

**Student:** Honestly the stress comes from uncertainty more than workload sometimes. Like even if the work is manageable, not knowing whether your group is aligned makes everything feel heavier.

**Interviewer:** That makes sense. Anything else before we wrap up?

**Student:** Just that students don't want "project management." They want less chaos. Those sound similar, but they feel really different.
