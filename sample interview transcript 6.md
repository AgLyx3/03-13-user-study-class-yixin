# User Interview Transcript 6

---

**Interviewer:** Thanks for doing this. Can you tell me about the last group project you worked on?

**Student:** Uh, yeah. It was for a systems class. I think there were five of us. Maybe four? Sorry, I remember the project more than the people count.

**Interviewer:** That's fine. What was the setup?

**Student:** We had a shared doc, a chat, and I think someone made a Notion page for like one day. So there were too many places where the truth could be.

**Interviewer:** "The truth"?

**Student:** Yeah, like the current state. Who owns what, what changed, whether something is actually done. In coding projects especially, something can look done and still be very not done.

**Interviewer:** How did your group usually communicate changes?

**Student:** Randomly. Sorry, that's not a useful answer. Mostly through text. Sometimes comments in the doc. Sometimes right before class. That's part of the problem.

**Interviewer:** No, that's useful. Can you give me a concrete example?

**Student:** One person updated part of the architecture plan in the doc, but then another person mentioned a different approach in the chat later that night. I saw the chat message but forgot to update my understanding, so the next day I was still working off the old version.

**Interviewer:** So the issue was forgetting?

**Student:** Me forgetting, yes. But also the group not having one place that made the latest decision obvious.

**Interviewer:** Got it.

**Student:** Also, and this is maybe separate, it was hard to know whether someone touching the doc meant they had finished their task or were just poking at it.

**Interviewer:** That's interesting. How did you usually find out?

**Student:** You ask in the chat and then wait. Or you assume. Neither is great.

**Interviewer:** Did people respond quickly?

**Student:** Sometimes. But if someone is in lab or class or just ignoring messages for a while, then now your progress depends on how interpretable the existing signals are.

**Interviewer:** "Interpretable signals" sounds like a CS answer.

**Student:** Yeah, sorry.

**Interviewer:** No, it's good. Keep going.

**Student:** I guess what I mean is there are lots of traces of activity, but not necessarily clarity. A comment, an edit, a message, a reaction emoji. None of those reliably tell you project state.

**Interviewer:** Did anyone take on the role of coordinating all that?

**Student:** Usually there is one person who's most organized, but even they become a bottleneck. Everyone starts asking them for the latest version of reality.

**Interviewer:** Was that you?

**Student:** Sometimes, yeah. Or half me and half one other person. Which is also bad because then even the fallback system is split.

**Interviewer:** That's good. Did your group try a project management tool?

**Student:** We sort of tried Notion. But it felt like homework on top of homework. Also, if the conversation still happens in chat, the Notion page becomes stale immediately.

**Interviewer:** So complexity is the problem.

**Student:** Complexity, but also separation. Even a simple tool can fail if it's not where the attention is.

**Interviewer:** That's a useful distinction.

**Student:** Yeah. If something existed in the same place we already talk, I'd be more likely to trust it at least at first.

**Interviewer:** Like a bot in the chat?

**Student:** Maybe. Depends what it does. If it's just noisy, no. If it can extract ownership, decisions, changes, maybe.

**Interviewer:** Would summaries help?

**Student:** Only if they are structured. Not "here's what happened in the chat." More like "here's the current state, here's what changed, here's what's unresolved."

**Interviewer:** Would you want it to assign work too?

**Student:** No. Suggest, maybe. Assign, no. Students would hate that.

**Interviewer:** What would make you trust it?

**Student:** It has to be right often enough that checking it saves time instead of creating another verification task. Also, it should make uncertainty visible instead of pretending everything is known.

**Interviewer:** That's a good point.

**Student:** Yeah. Honestly, a tool that confidently says the wrong thing is worse than no tool.

**Interviewer:** Anything else that feels important here?

**Student:** Just that messy group projects are not always messy because people are irresponsible. Sometimes the information architecture is just terrible.
